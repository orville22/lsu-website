# Hyponatraemia: Classification and Investigation

## Learning objectives

- Classify hyponatraemia by severity, duration and tonicity, and explain why each one changes what happens next.
- Use measured osmolality and glucose to exclude non-hypotonic hyponatraemia, and correct sodium for hyperglycaemia.
- Interpret a spot urine osmolality and sodium, collected with the blood sample, to narrow down the cause.
- State the essential criteria for the syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis (SIAD), including what must be excluded first.
- Explain why diuretics make urine sodium unreliable, and how fractional uric acid excretion helps.
- Describe the laboratory's part in safe correction: repeat testing, method consistency and the 24-hour limit.

## The core concepts

### 1. A low sodium is a water problem until proven otherwise

Sodium is a ratio: the amount of sodium divided by the water it's dissolved in (C1.1, concept 1). So a low sodium can mean too little sodium, too much water, or both. In most patients it's mainly too much water, held on to because antidiuretic hormone (ADH, also called vasopressin) is switched on.

Hyponatraemia is a serum sodium below 135 mmol/L, and it is the most common disorder of fluid and electrolyte balance in clinical practice [1]. Before chasing a cause, three labels help decide how urgent it is and how fast it can safely be corrected [1]:

Table: Classifying hyponatraemia

| Label | Definition | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mild | 130–135 mmol/L | Often found by chance; still worth a cause |
| Moderate | 125–129 mmol/L | Needs investigation and closer monitoring |
| Profound | below 125 mmol/L | Highest risk; repeat sodium often during treatment |
| Acute | documented for less than 48 h | Brain has not adapted; swelling is the danger |
| Chronic | at least 48 h, or unknown | Brain has adapted; correcting too fast is the danger |

The 48-hour line matters because brain cells defend their volume. When plasma tonicity falls, water moves into cells and they swell (C1.1, concept 2). Over the next day or two, brain cells shed solutes so they draw in less water. That protects them, but it also means a rapid rise in sodium can now pull water out of adapted cells and injure them: osmotic demyelination [2]. If nobody knows when the hyponatraemia started, the guideline says treat it as chronic [1].

> **Key idea**
> Severity says how worried to be. Duration says how fast you're allowed to fix it.

### 2. First question: is the plasma really hypotonic?

Only one kind of hyponatraemia moves water into cells: the hypotonic kind. Two others look the same on the report but aren't:

- **Pseudohyponatraemia.** The sodium in plasma water is normal, but excess lipid or protein makes the main analyser read it low. Measured osmolality is normal (C1.4).
- **Hypertonic (translocational) hyponatraemia.** Something else that stays outside cells, usually glucose without insulin, pulls water out of cells and dilutes the sodium. Osmolality is normal or high (C1.2, concept 4).

A measured osmolality below 275 mmol/kg always means hypotonic hyponatraemia [1]. The reverse doesn't hold. Urea and ethanol raise measured osmolality but cross cell membranes, so a patient can have a normal measured osmolality and still be hypotonic [3]. Read the urea and the ethanol before you call it non-hypotonic.

Choose "Glucose, no insulin": the cell shrinks and water moves out into the plasma, diluting the sodium. That's a real fall in sodium, but not a water excess, so it isn't treated like one.

**Correcting sodium for glucose.** The guideline adds 2.4 mmol/L to the measured sodium for every 5.5 mmol/L that glucose is above normal [1]. The factor comes from a study that raised glucose in healthy volunteers and watched sodium fall [4]. A patient with glucose 33 mmol/L and sodium 128 mmol/L is (33 − 5.5) ÷ 5.5 = 5 steps above normal, so the corrected sodium is 128 + 5 × 2.4 = 140 mmol/L. The low sodium is explained by the glucose.

> **Go deeper · the correction isn't a straight line**
> Hillier's volunteers didn't fall in a straight line. Sodium fell by about 1.6 mmol/L per 5.5 mmol/L of glucose at modest glucose, but closer to 4 mmol/L per step above about 22 mmol/L (400 mg/dL). The 2.4 figure is the best single average, not a law [4].

**Quick check.** Sodium 129 mmol/L, glucose 5 mmol/L, urea 30 mmol/L, measured osmolality 290 mmol/kg. Is this hypotonic? *Answer: yes. The urea raises the measured osmolality but doesn't hold water outside cells, so tonicity is low.*

### 3. Second question: what is the kidney doing with water?

Once you know the plasma is hypotonic, ask the kidney. A low plasma tonicity should switch ADH off, and with ADH off the kidney makes large volumes of dilute urine (C1.1, concept 3). So the urine osmolality tells you whether ADH is off, as it should be, or still on.

The guideline uses one threshold on a spot urine [1]:

- **Urine osmolality 100 mmol/kg or less:** ADH is off and the kidney is diluting as hard as it can. The water is simply coming in faster than it can go out. This is relative excess water intake: drinking very large volumes, or eating so little solute that there is too little to carry the water out.
- **Urine osmolality above 100 mmol/kg:** ADH is acting. The next question is whether that ADH is a reasonable response to a real stimulus, or inappropriate.

The low-solute version surprises people. Urine is water carrying solute out of the body, and even fully dilute urine needs some solute in every litre. Ellison and Berl put it simply: how much fluid a person can tolerate is proportional to their solute intake [5]. For illustration: someone excreting 300 mmol of solute a day at a urine osmolality of 100 mmol/kg can make at most 3 L of urine. Drink more than that on a diet of tea and toast, and the sodium falls even with ADH switched off.

> **Key idea**
> Urine osmolality asks one thing: is ADH on? At 100 mmol/kg or below, it's off, and the problem is intake.

### 4. Third question: is the kidney keeping sodium or losing it?

When ADH is on, the urine sodium, collected at the same time as the blood, helps explain why [1]:

- **Urine sodium 30 mmol/L or less:** the kidney is holding on to sodium. That points to a low effective arterial blood volume: true fluid loss from vomiting or diarrhoea, or the "underfilled arteries" of heart failure and cirrhosis (C1.1). In both, the ADH is a response to a real volume signal [1].
- **Urine sodium above 30 mmol/L:** the kidney is letting sodium go. The causes are diuretics, kidney disease, adrenal insufficiency and SIAD, and the next steps are the patient's volume status and their medicines [1].

Why not start with volume status, as older textbooks do? Because it's hard to judge at the bedside. In the studies the guideline reviewed, clinical assessment of volume had a sensitivity of only 0.5 to 0.8 and a specificity of 0.3 to 0.5 [1]. The urine tests are more objective, which is why both the European and US guidance lean on them [1,6].

**Proposed site visual · the three questions.** A decision path: is plasma hypotonic? → urine osmolality above 100? → urine sodium above 30? Each branch ends in a short list of causes, and every number on screen comes from a paired sample.

**Quick check.** Urine osmolality 450 mmol/kg and urine sodium 12 mmol/L in a patient with cirrhosis. Is ADH appropriate here? *Answer: it's a response to low effective arterial volume, which the low urine sodium supports. It isn't SIAD.*

### 5. SIAD: the diagnosis you make by exclusion

The syndrome of inappropriate antidiuresis is the most frequent cause of hyponatraemia [5,7]. ADH (or something that acts like it) keeps working although plasma tonicity is low and blood volume is normal. The kidney holds on to water and the sodium is diluted. Because salt intake and output stay in balance, the urine sodium is usually above 30 mmol/L, which is one of the diagnostic criteria [1,5].

There's no single test for SIAD. It's a diagnosis built from criteria, and the lab supplies almost all of them [1]:

Table: Diagnostic criteria for SIAD

| Essential criteria | Supplemental (supportive) features |
| --- | --- |
| Effective serum osmolality below 275 mmol/kg | Serum uric acid below 0.24 mmol/L |
| Urine osmolality above 100 mmol/kg | Serum urea below 3.6 mmol/L |
| Clinically euvolaemic | Fractional uric acid excretion of 12% or more |
| Urine sodium above 30 mmol/L on a normal salt and water intake | Fractional urea excretion of 55% or more |
| No adrenal, thyroid, pituitary or kidney insufficiency | Sodium doesn't correct with 0.9% saline |
| No recent diuretic use | Sodium corrects with fluid restriction |

The exclusions are the lab's job. Adrenal insufficiency can look exactly like SIAD and is treatable, so it must be considered before the label is applied [1]. Hypothyroidism is listed too, but it causes hyponatraemia only very rarely [1].

Common causes of SIAD fall into a few groups [5]:

- **Drugs:** selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), carbamazepine, cyclophosphamide, MDMA and others.
- **Malignancy:** small-cell lung cancer is the classic one; the syndrome was first described in patients with lung cancer.
- **Lung and brain disease:** pneumonia, tuberculosis, stroke, head injury, meningitis.
- **Other stimuli:** pain, nausea, general anaesthesia and surgery, and endurance exercise.

> **Go deeper · a low uric acid and urea**
> Serum uric acid and urea tend to be low in SIAD. A serum uric acid below 0.24 mmol/L (4 mg/dL) had a positive predictive value for SIAD of 73 to 100% in the studies Ellison and Berl reviewed, but neither feature is diagnostic on its own [5,1].

## The case

*Illustrative case; not a real patient.*

A GP rings the lab about a 68-year-old man. His wife says he has been more forgetful and unsteady for about a week. Three weeks ago he started sertraline for low mood. He takes no other regular medicines, and the GP thinks he looks well hydrated.

"His sodium's 121. Is this the sertraline? What else do you need from me?"

> **Lab report card**
> Serum: Sodium 121 mmol/L (135–145) · Potassium 4.1 mmol/L · Urea 2.8 mmol/L · Creatinine 62 µmol/L · Glucose 5.4 mmol/L · Uric acid 0.16 mmol/L · Measured osmolality 254 mmol/kg (275–295) · Total protein 70 g/L, sample not lipaemic. Spot urine, collected with the blood: osmolality 430 mmol/kg · sodium 64 mmol/L. Thyroid and adrenal function: requested.

Work through the three questions from the core concepts. The sections below add what you need to answer the GP properly.

## Supporting concepts

### Reading the paired urine

This table puts the three questions together. It is a guide to likely causes, not a diagnosis.

Table: A low sodium, read from a paired blood and urine sample

| Measured osmolality | Urine osmolality | Urine sodium | Most likely groups |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal, with a normal glucose | — | — | Pseudohyponatraemia: check lipids, protein and a direct sodium (C1.4) |
| Normal or high, glucose high | — | — | Hypertonic hyponatraemia: correct sodium for glucose |
| Low | 100 mmol/kg or less | — | Excess water intake, or low solute intake |
| Low | above 100 mmol/kg | 30 mmol/L or less | Low effective arterial volume: fluid loss, heart failure, cirrhosis |
| Low | above 100 mmol/kg | above 30 mmol/L | Diuretics, kidney disease, adrenal insufficiency, SIAD |

Two rules make the table work. The urine and blood must be collected together, because each only makes sense next to the other [1]. And a spot urine is enough: osmolality and sodium are best measured on the same spot sample [1].

### When the patient is on a diuretic

Diuretics make the kidney excrete sodium on purpose, so a urine sodium above 30 mmol/L no longer means much. That's a real problem, because thiazide and thiazide-like diuretics are among the main drug causes of hyponatraemia. A systematic review of published cases describes them as the leading cause of drug-induced hyponatraemia needing admission [8]. Patients were typically older women, presenting on average 19 days after starting the drug, with a mean sodium of 116 mmol/L and a mildly low potassium [8].

Fractional uric acid excretion (FEUA) helps here. It's calculated from paired serum and urine results:

$$\text{FEUA (\%)} = \frac{\text{urine urate} \times \text{serum creatinine}}{\text{serum urate} \times \text{urine creatinine}} \times 100$$

Use the same units for each pair, so that creatinine and urate both cancel. In a study of 86 consecutive hyponatraemic patients, FEUA was the best of the markers tested in those taking diuretics: above a cut-off of 12%, its positive predictive value for SIAD was 100%. Combined with urine sodium, it diagnosed SIAD accurately with or without diuretics [7]. It's one of the guideline's supplemental criteria [1].

> **Go deeper · copeptin**
> ADH itself is difficult to measure reliably. Copeptin, a fragment released alongside it, is easier to measure. Hoorn and Zietse list it with FEUA as a recent development that may improve the diagnostic approach, but it isn't yet part of either guideline's pathway [6].

### Correcting safely: the lab sets the pace

Treatment is the clinician's decision, but it runs on sodium results, and the timing of those results is the lab's responsibility.

- **Severe symptoms.** The European guideline recommends 150 mL of 3% hypertonic saline over 20 minutes, with a sodium checked after 20 minutes. This can be repeated twice, or until the sodium has risen by 5 mmol/L [1]. Each repeat sample is time-critical.
- **The limit.** In chronic hyponatraemia, the guideline advises keeping the rise to no more than 10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours and 8 mmol/L in each 24 hours after that [1]. Faster correction risks osmotic demyelination [2,9].
- **Monitoring.** For moderate or profound hyponatraemia, the guideline suggests a sodium every 6 hours until it stabilises [1].

> **From the bench · keep the method the same**
> A patient being corrected may have one sodium from the main analyser and the next from a blood gas analyser. Those use different methods, and in samples with abnormal protein they can disagree by 4 mmol/L or more [10]. Even two direct methods can differ enough to matter when the margin is tight [11]. A 4 mmol/L method difference can look like 4 mmol/L of correction. Where you can, monitor on one method, and say on the report when a result came from a different one.

### In the lab

Table: Investigating hyponatraemia in the laboratory

| Step | What to do | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirm it | Repeat if unexpected; look at the sample | Lipaemia or very high protein: think pseudohyponatraemia (C1.4) |
| Tonicity | Measured osmolality by freezing point (C1.2); glucose with every sodium | Urea and ethanol raise osmolality without raising tonicity [3] |
| Glucose | Correct sodium: + 2.4 mmol/L per 5.5 mmol/L of glucose above normal [1] | The factor is an average; very high glucose bends it [4] |
| Paired urine | Spot urine osmolality and sodium, collected with the blood [1] | A urine from a different day is uninterpretable |
| Exclusions | Thyroid and adrenal function before SIAD is named [1] | Adrenal insufficiency mimics SIAD and is treatable |
| Diuretics | Add urine urate and creatinine for FEUA [7] | Urine sodium is unreliable on diuretics |
| Monitoring | Sodium every 6 h in moderate or profound cases [1] | Rise of more than 10 mmol/L in 24 h: phone it [1] |

> **Pre-analytical trap · the urine sent tomorrow**
> A urine collected the next morning, after a night of intravenous fluids or a first dose of saline, describes a different patient. Ask for the urine to be collected with the blood, before treatment starts, wherever possible [1].

## Cracking the case

Work through the questions in order.

**Is the plasma hypotonic?** Yes. The measured osmolality is 254 mmol/kg, well below 275 [1]. Glucose is normal, the sample isn't lipaemic, and protein is normal, so there's no pseudo- or hypertonic hyponatraemia to explain away.

**Is ADH on?** Yes. A urine osmolality of 430 mmol/kg in someone with a plasma osmolality of 254 is far more concentrated than it should be. A healthy kidney would be making dilute urine.

**Keeping sodium or losing it?** Losing it: urine sodium is 64 mmol/L. He's not on a diuretic, and the GP finds him euvolaemic. That's the SIAD pattern [1].

**The supporting features agree.** Urea is 2.8 mmol/L (below 3.6) and uric acid is 0.16 mmol/L (below 0.24), both supplemental features of SIAD [1,5]. Sertraline, an SSRI started three weeks ago, is a recognised cause [5].

So the honest answer to the GP has three parts:

- **What the lab can say:** this is hypotonic hyponatraemia with inappropriately concentrated urine and a high urine sodium, the pattern of SIAD, and an SSRI is a likely cause.
- **What it can't say yet:** that it *is* SIAD. Thyroid and adrenal function must come back normal first, because adrenal insufficiency looks the same and is treated differently [1].
- **What happens next:** at 121 mmol/L this is profound, and with no known start date it counts as chronic [1]. Wherever he's treated, his sodium should be checked often, and a rise of more than 10 mmol/L in the first 24 hours should be phoned through [1].

> **The principle to take with you**
> Work a low sodium in order: is it real and hypotonic, is ADH on, and is the kidney keeping or losing sodium? SIAD is where you land after the other doors are shut.

## Key takeaways

- Hyponatraemia is a sodium below 135 mmol/L; classify it by severity (mild, moderate, profound) and duration (acute under 48 h, otherwise chronic).
- First exclude pseudohyponatraemia and hypertonic hyponatraemia; correct sodium by 2.4 mmol/L for every 5.5 mmol/L of glucose above normal.
- A measured osmolality below 275 mmol/kg always means hypotonic; a normal one doesn't exclude it if urea or ethanol is high.
- Urine osmolality of 100 mmol/kg or less means ADH is off: think excess water or low solute intake.
- With ADH on, a urine sodium of 30 mmol/L or less points to low effective arterial volume; above 30 points to diuretics, kidney or adrenal disease, or SIAD.
- SIAD needs euvolaemia, no diuretics, and normal thyroid and adrenal function; low urea, low urate and FEUA of 12% or more support it.
- In chronic hyponatraemia, sodium should rise by no more than 10 mmol/L in the first 24 h; repeat sodiums on the same method.

## Try it

**Q1 · multiple choice.** Which result, on its own, confirms that a hyponatraemia is hypotonic?

- A. Urine osmolality above 100 mmol/kg
- B. Measured serum osmolality of 262 mmol/kg
- C. Urine sodium below 30 mmol/L
- D. A normal glucose

*Answer:* B. A measured osmolality below 275 mmol/kg always reflects hypotonic hyponatraemia [1].

**Q2 · calculation.** Sodium 124 mmol/L, glucose 38.5 mmol/L. Correct the sodium for glucose.

*Model answer:* (38.5 − 5.5) ÷ 5.5 = 6 steps. 124 + 6 × 2.4 = 138.4 mmol/L. The low sodium is explained by the glucose [1].

**Q3 · multiple choice.** A woman with low intake of food has sodium 126 mmol/L, measured osmolality 262 mmol/kg and urine osmolality 70 mmol/kg. What's the most likely mechanism?

- A. SIAD
- B. Thiazide diuretic
- C. Excess water relative to solute intake, with ADH appropriately off
- D. Pseudohyponatraemia

*Answer:* C. A urine osmolality of 100 mmol/kg or less means maximally dilute urine, so ADH is off and intake outruns excretion [1,5].

**Q4 · calculation.** A patient on a thiazide has urine urate 2.0 mmol/L, urine creatinine 5.0 mmol/L, serum urate 0.15 mmol/L and serum creatinine 0.070 mmol/L (70 µmol/L). Calculate the FEUA and interpret it.

*Model answer:* (2.0 × 0.070) ÷ (0.15 × 5.0) × 100 = 0.14 ÷ 0.75 × 100 ≈ 19%. Above 12%, which favours SIAD even though the urine sodium can't be used on a diuretic [7].

**Q5 · short answer.** In two or three sentences, explain why a laboratory should be cautious about switching between the main analyser and a blood gas analyser while a patient's hyponatraemia is being corrected.

*Model answer:* The two use different methods (indirect and direct ISE), which can disagree by 4 mmol/L or more when protein is abnormal [10]. A method change can look like correction, or hide it, when the safe limit is 10 mmol/L in 24 hours [1]. Monitoring on one method keeps the trend honest.

**Q6 · multiple choice.** Which must be excluded before SIAD can be diagnosed?

- A. Hyperuricaemia
- B. Adrenal insufficiency
- C. A urine osmolality above 100 mmol/kg
- D. A low urea

*Answer:* B. Adrenal, thyroid, pituitary and kidney insufficiency must be absent; adrenal insufficiency is the one that most often mimics SIAD [1].
