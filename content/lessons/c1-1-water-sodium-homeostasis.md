# Water and Sodium Homeostasis: ADH, the RAAS and Thirst

## Learning objectives

- Explain why serum sodium reflects water balance, not how much sodium the body holds.
- Describe where water, sodium and potassium sit in the body, and why water moves between compartments.
- Explain how antidiuretic hormone (ADH) and thirst control water, and how the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and natriuretic peptides control sodium.
- Explain why a patient with oedema can still have a low sodium.
- Read paired plasma osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium to see which systems are switched on.
- Handle copeptin, renin and aldosterone samples correctly.

## The core concepts

Sodium is the main positively charged ion (cation) in the fluid outside our cells. In a healthy adult, serum sodium sits between 135 and 145 mmol/L, the harmonised reference interval used across Australasian laboratories [1]. Inside cells it is far lower, because a pump in every cell membrane, the Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase, keeps moving it out [2]. Concept 2 shows why that split matters.

Sodium matters because water follows it. Together with its partner anions, chloride and bicarbonate, it makes up most of the dissolved particles in plasma (C1.2). So it largely decides how much water stays in the blood and the fluid around cells, and which way water moves between them [3]. That's why sodium is one of the most requested tests in the lab, and why a low sodium (hyponatraemia) is the most common disorder of fluid and electrolyte balance in clinical practice [4]. This lesson explains how the body holds sodium steady, and what the number on the report is really telling you.

### 1. The lab measures concentration, not amount

The lab doesn't measure how much sodium your body holds. It measures sodium **concentration**: how much is dissolved in each litre of plasma. That means the result can move for two different reasons: the amount of sodium changes, or the amount of water does.

If it helps, picture a jug of cordial. Add more syrup and the drink gets stronger. Add more water and it gets weaker, even though all the syrup is still there. Sodium in plasma behaves the same way.

@interactive(c1-1-concentration "C1.1 interactive I1 · amount versus concentration")

Try the heart failure preset. The body holds more sodium than normal, yet the result is low, because the water rose even more. You'll meet a patient like this shortly.

Serum sodium follows the ratio of exchangeable sodium (plus potassium) to total body water. Exchangeable means free to move: some of the body's sodium is locked away in bone and doesn't count [3,5]. Why potassium is in there comes in concept 2. Sodium content sets the volume of the extracellular fluid (ECF), and water balance sets the concentration. That's because sodium is held outside cells, and water follows it to keep the concentration of dissolved particles equal on both sides, so the amount of sodium decides how much water the ECF holds [3].

> **Key idea**
> A low sodium usually means too much water, not too little sodium.

**Quick check.** A patient retains 2 L of water with no change in body sodium. What happens to the lab result, and to the sodium in their body? *Answer: the result falls; the body's sodium is unchanged.*

> **Go deeper · where the idea came from**
> In 1958, Edelman and colleagues measured exchangeable sodium, potassium and total body water with isotopes in 98 patients. Serum sodium tracked their ratio [5]. Modern reviews still use a simplified form of their equation [3].

### 2. Where water and sodium live

Water makes up about 60% of body weight in men and about 50% in women, a little less in older adults [6]. It isn't one pool. It's split between the fluid inside cells (intracellular fluid, ICF) and the fluid outside them (extracellular fluid, ECF).

You may have learnt the classic split of two-thirds inside and one-third outside. A detailed reanalysis puts it closer to 57% inside and 43% outside in an average adult man [6]. The ECF includes the interstitial fluid that bathes the cells and the plasma the lab actually samples, as well as smaller pools in bone, connective tissue and body cavities [6].

@interactive(c1-1-compartments "C1.1 visual 2 · where water, sodium and potassium live")

Every cell is wrapped in a **semi-permeable** (selectively permeable) membrane. Water crosses it easily, largely through water channels called aquaporins [7]. Sodium, potassium and most other solutes cannot cross on their own; they move only through specific channels and transporters. Some of that movement is **passive**, running down a concentration gradient without using energy. Some is **active**, using energy from ATP to move ions against their gradient [2].

The two sides hold different salts. Sodium is mostly outside cells and potassium mostly inside. The sodium pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase) in every cell membrane keeps it that way: it uses energy to push three sodium ions out for every two potassium ions it brings in [2,3]. Water is different: it crosses cell membranes freely and moves by **osmosis**, the net movement of water across a semi-permeable membrane towards the side with more dissolved particles, until both sides match. So the solute concentration inside and outside cells stays equal [3].

That has a direct consequence. If the ECF becomes more dilute, water moves into cells and they swell; if it becomes more concentrated, water leaves and they shrink. This effect of plasma on cell volume is called **tonicity** [3]. It's why sodium disorders show up as neurological symptoms: brain cells are swelling or shrinking [3].

> **Key term · osmolality**
> Osmolality is the concentration of all the dissolved particles in a kilogram of water, in mmol/kg. In plasma, sodium and its partner anions make up most of it. C1.2 covers how the lab measures it.

@interactive(c1-1-cell-volume "C1.1 visual · cells swell and shrink")

> **Go deeper · where the cordial analogy breaks**
> Your body isn't one jug. Potassium inside cells draws water in too, which is why potassium belongs in the sodium-to-water ratio. It's also why replacing potassium raises serum sodium much as giving sodium would [3].

**Quick check.** Plasma becomes more dilute. Which way does water move, and what happens to the cells? *Answer: into the cells, which swell.*

### 3. Two control systems: one for water, one for sodium

Concentration and amount are different things, so the body controls them separately, using two systems that each have their own sensors, hormones and kidney actions:

- **The water system** watches tonicity. Its tools are antidiuretic hormone (ADH, also called vasopressin or arginine vasopressin, AVP) and thirst, and it sets the sodium concentration.
- **The sodium system** watches how well the arteries are filled, a quantity called the effective arterial blood volume (concept 5 explains it). Its tools are the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and the natriuretic peptides, and it sets the volume of the ECF.

@interactive(c1-1-two-systems "C1.1 visual 3 · the two control systems")

Most of the time the two systems work side by side without conflict. The highlighted arrow is the exception, and it's the key to the case later in this lesson. The next two concepts look at each system in turn.

### 4. The water system: ADH and thirst

**The sensors.** Specialised neurons called osmoreceptors sit in circumventricular organs of the brain, small areas outside the blood–brain barrier, so they can sense the plasma directly [8]. When plasma becomes more concentrated, these cells, and the ADH-making cells themselves, shrink slightly. The shrinking opens stretch-sensitive channels and makes them fire, and that is the signal [9].

**The two responses.** They trigger release of ADH from the posterior pituitary, and they make you thirsty [8]. ADH saves water you already have; thirst brings in more. Osmolality isn't ADH's only trigger: a large fall in blood volume switches it on too, which you'll see in the interactive in concept 6 and again in the case.

**How ADH works.** ADH binds V2 receptors on the principal cells of the collecting duct. This moves aquaporin-2 water channels from vesicles inside the cell into the membrane facing the urine [3,7]. An open channel only lets water move if something pulls it. The pull comes from the inner kidney: the loops of Henle pump salt into the tissue of the medulla, so it gets saltier with depth, from about 300 mmol/kg near the cortex to about 1,200 mmol/kg at the tip [10]. The collecting duct runs down through that salty tissue, so water leaves the urine through the open channels, crosses the cell and exits through aquaporin-3 and -4 on the blood side [7,10]. With ADH high, the urine becomes concentrated. With ADH suppressed, the channels are withdrawn and the kidney excretes dilute urine, clearing free water: water passed without extra solute, which dilutes the urine.

@interactive(c1-1-nephron-adh "C1.1 visual · where the kidney takes water back")

@interactive(c1-1-adh-water "C1.1 visual · how ADH gets water back")

**How sensitive it is.** The system responds to changes in osmolality of only 1–2% [11,12]. In human studies ADH switches on at about 284 mmol/kg, with thirst starting at a similar point [13]. Because sodium and its partner anions make up most of osmolality (C1.2), the switch-on point can be put in sodium terms: ADH becomes detectable once plasma sodium rises above about 135 mmol/L, and by about 145 mmol/L the urine is maximally concentrated, near 1,200 mmol/kg [3]. That's the sodium reference interval, seen from the hormone's side.

@interactive(c1-1-adh-switch "C1.1 visual 4 · the ADH switch")

**Why thirst matters so much.** The kidney can only save water, not make it. Thirst is what replaces the water lost each day, and it usually prevents a high sodium even when ADH is completely absent [3].

**Quick check.** Plasma osmolality is low. What urine would you expect if this system were in charge? *Answer: maximally dilute urine, because ADH should be switched off.*

> **Go deeper · the thirst threshold debate**
> Early studies placed the thirst threshold well above ADH's, near 298 mmol/kg [12]. Later work using visual analogue scales and regression found the two thresholds very similar, around 281–287 mmol/kg [13]. The difference is mostly method: when you ask, and how you measure thirst.

> **Go deeper · why water quenches thirst before it's absorbed**
> Thirst isn't driven by osmolality alone. Signals from the mouth, throat and gut tell the brain that fluid is on its way, and they switch thirst off before plasma osmolality has changed [8].

### 5. The sodium system: the RAAS and natriuretic peptides

**What it watches.** The sodium system does not measure total body fluid. It measures how well the arteries are filled, a quantity called the effective arterial blood volume [14]. A patient can carry litres of extra fluid in their tissues and still have underfilled arteries. The sensors are baroreceptors, stretch sensors in the walls of the carotid sinus, the aortic arch and the left ventricle. They signal to the brain and, through the sympathetic nerves, to the kidney [14,15]. Fluid that has leaked out of the capillaries into the tissues sits outside the circulation, where these sensors can't count it [14,16].

@interactive(c1-1-underfilled-arteries "C1.1 visual · full tissues, underfilled arteries")

**The cascade.** When filling falls, the kidney releases renin (how that happens is below). Renin cuts angiotensinogen, made by the liver, into angiotensin I, and angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) turns that into angiotensin II [17].

**What triggers renin release.** The juxtaglomerular (JG) cells sit in the wall of the afferent arteriole, right where blood enters the glomerulus. They release renin in response to three signals [14,17]:

@interactive(c1-1-jg-triggers "C1.1 visual · what switches on renin release")

- **Pressure.** A fall in blood pressure reaching the kidney triggers renin release directly [17].
- **Salt delivery.** The macula densa, a patch of tubule cells next to the JG cells, senses how much sodium chloride is reaching the distal tubule. When less sodium chloride arrives, it signals the JG cells to release renin [17].
- **Nerves.** When arterial filling falls, the sympathetic nervous system is activated, and sympathetic signals also prompt renin release [14,17].

In a real scenario, such as blood loss or a weak heart, the three signals tend to arrive together. That's why the RAAS responds so strongly when the circulation is underfilled.

@interactive(c1-1-raas-cascade "C1.1 visual 5 · the RAAS cascade")

**What angiotensin II does.** It drives aldosterone release from the adrenal cortex [17]. It also stimulates thirst and ADH release, so it brings in water as well as keeping salt [8]. Together with the sympathetic nervous system and ADH, it also constricts blood vessels to support blood pressure in the short term [14].

**What aldosterone does.** It acts on principal cells in the last part of the nephron, increasing the activity of epithelial sodium channels (ENaC). More sodium is reabsorbed from the urine, and potassium and hydrogen ions are secreted in exchange [17]. Water follows the retained sodium, but not by itself. Retained sodium raises osmolality slightly; the osmoreceptors detect the rise, ADH and thirst respond, and the additional water restores the concentration. The ECF expands while serum sodium barely changes. That's why the sodium system sets volume, not concentration [3].

@interactive(c1-1-water-follows-sodium "C1.1 visual · water follows sodium")

**The counterweight.** When the heart's chambers are stretched by too much volume, they release atrial and B-type natriuretic peptides (ANP and BNP), which push the kidney to excrete sodium [18].

> **Go deeper · what the natriuretic peptides do**
> ANP and BNP raise the kidney's filtration rate, damp down renin and aldosterone, and reduce sodium reabsorption in the tubule, so more sodium leaves in the urine [18]. BNP and its fragment NT-proBNP are measured in the lab to help diagnose heart failure [18].

**Quick check.** Which system would you expect to respond first after a very salty meal? *Answer: the water system. Osmolality rises, so ADH and thirst respond first; the extra sodium is then excreted as the ECF expands and the RAAS is switched down.*

> **Go deeper · why aldosterone also matters for potassium**
> Aldosterone secretion responds to potassium as well as angiotensin II, and its action on principal cells swaps sodium for potassium [17]. That link returns in C2.1 and C2.2.

Before you meet a patient, see how the two systems handle everyday challenges. Pick a situation and watch which sensor detects the change, which hormones respond and what the kidney does about it.

Notice the pattern. Changes in osmolality are handled mainly by ADH and thirst; changes in arterial filling mainly by the RAAS and natriuretic peptides. Blood loss is the exception worth remembering: with no osmotic signal at all, ADH still switches on, because volume takes priority. You'll need that idea for the case.

## The case

*Illustrative case; not a real patient.*

The ward rings the lab. Their patient, a 74-year-old man with heart failure, has pitting oedema to both knees and has put on 4 kg in two weeks. His sodium is **125 mmol/L**.

"He's clearly holding on to salt and water," the registrar says. "So why is his sodium low? Does he need sodium?"

> **Lab report card**
> Sodium 125 mmol/L (135–145) · Potassium 4.1 mmol/L · Urea 11.2 mmol/L · Glucose 6.1 mmol/L · Measured osmolality 270 mmol/kg (275–295) · Spot urine osmolality 410 mmol/kg · Spot urine sodium 12 mmol/L. Blood and urine were collected together, before any diuretic dose.

You already have most of the pieces. Concept 1 tells you a low sodium can sit alongside extra body sodium. But two numbers on the card don't fit the systems as you've just met them. The sections below explain why.

## Supporting concepts

### When the two systems conflict, volume takes priority

Start with the first number that doesn't fit. His plasma osmolality is 270 mmol/kg, well below the ADH switch-on point, so the water system should have ADH switched off and his urine should be dilute. Instead his urine osmolality is 410 mmol/kg.

@interactive(c1-1-adh-switch-patient "C1.1 visual 6 · the ADH switch, applied to our patient")

Something other than osmolality is driving ADH. That something is volume. When the two systems conflict, the body protects its circulation first and its sodium concentration second.

The classic experiment showed this in rats. Once blood volume fell by about 8%, ADH rose steeply, and the osmotic switch-on point moved lower [19]. A volume signal can keep ADH switched on at an osmolality that would normally switch it off. This is called non-osmotic ADH release.

Now the second number: urine sodium 12 mmol/L. That's the sodium system at work, holding on to every bit of sodium it can.

Heart failure explains both. The weak pump leaves the arteries underfilled, even though the body is carrying extra fluid. Both systems respond as if the patient were dehydrated [14,20]:

@interactive(c1-1-heart-failure "C1.1 visual 7 · why heart failure lowers sodium")

Angiotensin II's push on thirst (concept 5) adds to the water load [8,20].

**Quick check.** Why does a patient with oedema keep releasing ADH when his plasma osmolality is low? *Answer: its baroreceptors sense underfilled arteries, and that volume signal overrides the osmotic signal to switch ADH off.*

> **Go deeper · measuring ADH through copeptin**
> ADH is hard to measure, so labs use copeptin, a fragment cut from the same precursor molecule that is stable after collection [21]. In 144 patients with hypotonic polyuria, a copeptin above 4.9 pmol/L after hypertonic saline gave the right diagnosis in 96.5% of cases, against 76.6% for the classic water deprivation test [22]. C1.5 covers this in detail.

### Reading the results

Faced with a low sodium, ask two separate questions: is this really a water problem, and what are the hormones doing? A spot urine taken alongside the blood answers most of it. The European hyponatraemia guideline uses these cut-offs [4]:

@interactive(c1-1-spot-urine "C1.1 visual 8 · reading the spot urine")

Two cautions. A diuretic dose before the urine was collected raises urine sodium and can send you down the wrong branch [4]. And the urine never replaces looking at the patient: reviews still group hyponatraemia by clinical volume status for good reason [23].

**Worked example: how much water does it take?** A 70 kg man has a sodium of 140 mmol/L. Assume his total body water is 60% of body weight, so 42 L [6]. He retains 2 L of water and loses no sodium.

$$140 \times \frac{42}{42 + 2} \approx 134\ \mathrm{mmol/L}$$

Two litres, about 3% of his body weight, takes him below the reference interval without a single sodium ion lost. This quick form ignores some refinements of the full equation, so treat it as an estimate [3,5].

### In the lab

The hormones in this lesson are hard to measure directly, so the lab mostly reads their footprints. Each test below is listed with what it tells you and its common pitfalls.

Table: Tests for water and sodium balance

| Test | What it tells you | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sodium | The solute-to-water ratio (concept 1) | Main analysers use an indirect ion-selective electrode (ISE), which dilutes the sample and assumes plasma is about 93% water; blood gas analysers use a direct ISE, which doesn't (C1.2, C1.4) [24]. Australasian harmonised adult interval 135–145 mmol/L [1] |
| Plasma osmolality | Whether plasma is truly hypotonic | Measured by freezing point depression; compare with calculated osmolality (C1.2) |
| Urine osmolality | Whether ADH is acting on the kidney | Collect the spot urine at the same time as the blood |
| Urine sodium | Whether the kidney is holding sodium | Diuretics push it up and can mislead (C1.6) |
| ADH | The water hormone itself | Unstable after collection and often below assay detection limits, so rarely requested [21,25] |
| Copeptin | A stand-in for ADH | Stable for at least 7 days at room temperature, with less than 20% loss [21] |
| Renin and aldosterone | Whether the RAAS is switched on | Collection conditions and medicines change the result (C9.4) [26] |

> **Pre-analytical trap · renin on ice**
> Chilling a renin sample turns inactive prorenin into active renin in the tube. This is *cryoactivation*, and it falsely raises renin [27]. Keep renin samples at room temperature until the plasma is separated and frozen [27]. Time of day, posture and interfering medicines must also be controlled before the aldosterone/renin ratio can be read [26].

> **From the bench**
> When collecting a sample for renin, use a dedicated tube and keep it at room temperature. Send it to the lab for processing as soon as possible. If it can't be analysed promptly, separate the plasma and freeze it.

## Cracking the case

Back to the registrar's question: how can a man holding too much sodium have a low sodium?

Because he's holding even more water. His weak heart leaves his arteries underfilled, so both systems behave as if he were dehydrated. Every number on the lab report card fits:

- **Plasma osmolality 270 mmol/kg:** the plasma really is dilute, so this is a true water excess.
- **Urine osmolality 410 mmol/kg:** ADH is switched on despite that dilute plasma, driven by the volume signal [14,20].
- **Urine sodium 12 mmol/L:** the RAAS has the kidney holding on to sodium [4,14].

Giving him sodium would only add to his oedema. Treatment targets the heart failure and the water excess, not a sodium shortage he doesn't have [20,23].

> **The principle to take with you**
> A concentration is a ratio. Before you act on one, ask whether the solute changed or the water did.

## Key takeaways

- The lab reports sodium concentration, a ratio of sodium to water, not how much sodium the body holds.
- Sodium lives mostly outside cells and potassium inside; water crosses freely, so cells swell or shrink with tonicity.
- Water sets the concentration (ADH and thirst); sodium sets the ECF volume (the RAAS and natriuretic peptides).
- ADH opens aquaporin-2 channels so the kidney keeps water; aldosterone increases ENaC activity so the kidney keeps sodium.
- When arterial filling falls, volume takes priority: non-osmotic ADH stays on even when plasma is dilute.
- Oedema with a low sodium means extra sodium and even more extra water.
- Read plasma osmolality, urine osmolality and urine sodium together, and keep renin samples off ice.

## Try it

**Q1 · multiple choice.** A patient's plasma becomes more dilute over a few hours. What happens to their brain cells?

- A. They shrink
- B. They swell
- C. Nothing, because sodium can't enter cells
- D. Nothing, because the sodium pump compensates

*Answer:* B. **Water crosses cell membranes freely**, so when the ECF becomes dilute, water moves into cells and they swell [3].

**Q2 · multiple choice.** Which hormone mainly controls how much water the kidney keeps?

- A. Aldosterone
- B. Atrial natriuretic peptide
- C. Antidiuretic hormone (ADH)
- D. Angiotensin II

*Answer:* C. ADH opens **aquaporin-2** channels in the collecting duct [7]. Aldosterone and the natriuretic peptides act mainly on sodium [17,18].

**Q3 · calculation.** A 60 kg woman has a sodium of 138 mmol/L and about 30 L of total body water. She retains 1.5 L of water and loses no sodium. Estimate her new sodium.

*Model answer:* 138 × 30 / 31.5 ≈ 131 mmol/L. Her body sodium hasn't changed; **only the water has** [3,5].

**Q4 · read the results (short answer).** Plasma osmolality 270 mmol/kg, urine osmolality 410 mmol/kg, urine sodium 12 mmol/L, no diuretic given. Which systems are switched on, and what does that suggest?

*Model answer:* ADH is on despite dilute plasma, so the trigger is **non-osmotic**. The low urine sodium shows the RAAS holding sodium. Together they point to **low effective arterial blood volume**, as in heart failure, cirrhosis or volume depletion [4,14].

**Q5 · short answer.** In two or three sentences, explain how a patient with oedema can have a low sodium.

*Model answer:* Underfilled arteries switch on the RAAS and non-osmotic ADH. The kidney keeps sodium, causing oedema, but **keeps even more water**, so the sodium-to-water ratio falls [14,19,20].

**Q6 · multiple choice.** A renin sample arrives sitting in ice water. What's the main risk?

- A. Falsely low renin from degradation
- B. Falsely high renin from cryoactivation of prorenin
- C. Haemolysis releasing renin from red cells
- D. No effect, because chilling is recommended

*Answer:* B. Cold converts **prorenin to active renin** in the tube. Keep renin samples at **room temperature** until separated and frozen [27].
